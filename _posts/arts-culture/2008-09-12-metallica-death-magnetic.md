**Key figures**: Metallica (James Hetfield, Lars Ulrich, Kirk Hammett, Robert Trujillo); Rick Rubin (producer)

## Summary

Metallica released *Death Magnetic* on September 12, 2008—their ninth studio album and the first to feature bassist Robert Trujillo, who had joined the band in February 2003 following the acrimonious departure of Jason Newsted. Produced by Rick Rubin—who had previously produced Slayer's *Reign in Blood* (1986), Red Hot Chili Peppers' *Blood Sugar Sex Magik* (1991), and Audioslave's debut (2002)—it was Metallica's first album not produced by Bob Rock since 1988 and represented the band's most deliberate and explicit return to the thrash-metal sound that had made them superstars between 1983 and 1991.

### Background: Metallica's Mid-Career Controversies

The album arrived against a backdrop of significant internal and reputational turmoil. *Load* (1996) and *ReLoad* (1997) had alienated traditionalist fans with their Southern rock and alternative metal influences. The 2003 documentary *Some Kind of Monster* had laid bare the band's interpersonal dysfunction and near-dissolution. The *St. Anger* album (2003)—produced by Bob Rock and recorded while the band worked with performance coach Phil Towle—received harsh criticism for its unorthodox production choices (the clanging snare drum sound, the absence of guitar solos). By 2008 there was genuine industry uncertainty about whether Metallica could regain critical and fan credibility.

Rick Rubin's involvement was seen as a deliberate course correction. Rubin had a reputation for stripping artists back to their essentials and removing studio overproduction. Sessions began in 2006 at the band's own HQ Studio in San Rafael, California, and recording continued through 2007. Hetfield, Ulrich, and Hammett co-wrote the material collaboratively, with Hetfield taking primary vocal and lyrical responsibility and Hammett returning to the guitar solos that had been eliminated from *St. Anger*.

### Tracks and Running Time

*Death Magnetic* featured ten tracks with a total running time of 74 minutes, making it one of Metallica's longest studio albums—a throwback to the extended compositions of *…And Justice for All* (1988). Standout tracks included:

- **"That Was Just Your Life"** (track 1, 7:10) — an aggressive opener echoing "Battery" from *Master of Puppets* (1986), built around a fast chromatic riff.
- **"All Nightmare Long"** (track 5, 8:01) — widely regarded as one of the strongest songs on the album, featuring intricate riffwork and an extended instrumental build.
- **"The Unforgiven III"** (track 7, 7:47) — third installment in the *Unforgiven* trilogy, a piano- and orchestration-led ballad.
- **"Cyanide"** (track 6, 6:41) — a mid-tempo track with an unusual rhythmic alternation.
- **"My Apocalypse"** (track 10, 5:01) — a high-speed thrash closer written deliberately to evoke *…And Justice for All*-era material.
- **"Suicide & Redemption"** (track 9, 9:57) — a full instrumental track, the first since "To Live Is to Die" in 1988.

### Commercial Performance

*Death Magnetic* debuted at number one in more than 30 countries, including the United States (Billboard 200) and United Kingdom (UK Albums Chart). In the United States it sold approximately 490,000 copies in its first week—the largest first-week Metallica album sales since *Load* (1996). Globally it had sold over 5 million copies by 2010. The lead single, "The Day That Never Comes," was released on August 21, 2008, followed by "All Nightmare Long" (December 2008) and "Broken, Beat & Scarred" (April 2009), while "My Apocalypse" and "Cyanide" circulated as promotional singles. At the 51st Grammy Awards (February 2009) the track "My Apocalypse" won Best Metal Performance.

## The Loudness War Controversy

*Death Magnetic* became a defining example of early 21st-century audio mastering malpractice. The album was mastered by Ted Jensen at Sterling Sound with extreme dynamic range compression applied during mixing by producer Rubin and engineer Greg Fidelman—resulting in a final product with a DR (dynamic range) score of approximately 3–4 on the EBUR128 scale, compared to the DR13–15 typical of 1980s metal recordings. Many listeners reported the album sounding "clipped"—audible distortion occurring when audio signals exceed 0 dBFS—particularly at high volume.

This provoked a rare public backlash. Fans on the Steve Hoffman Music Forums posted side-by-side audio comparisons showing waveforms that appeared as solid rectangular blocks rather than dynamic peaks and troughs. Ted Jensen himself published a statement distancing himself from the mastering decisions, saying the mixes he received were already heavily limited. An unusual point of comparison followed: when *Death Magnetic* was released as downloadable content for Guitar Hero III on the album's release day, those files derived from less-compressed pre-mastering mixes with markedly higher dynamic range. Fans who extracted the Guitar Hero audio noted it sounded noticeably better than the commercial CD release—a widely circulated comparison that fueled the broader "loudness war" debate.

This controversy accelerated discussions about the need for streaming services and broadcasters to implement loudness normalization standards (eventually embodied in the EBU R 128 and LUFS standards adopted between 2010 and 2015).

## Significance

*Death Magnetic* arrived on September 12, 2008—three days before the [collapse of Lehman Brothers]({{ '/news/society-economics/lehman-brothers-collapse/' | relative_url }}) and the onset of the acute phase of the [2008 financial crisis]({{ '/news/society-economics/2008-financial-crisis/' | relative_url }}). The timing was incidental, but critics noted that the album's themes of anger, struggle, and apocalyptic dread resonated with the cultural anxiety of late 2008. Metal's cyclical resurgence frequently coincides with periods of broad social frustration, and *Death Magnetic* was no exception.

The album revitalized Metallica's cultural standing after nearly a decade of declining relevance and proved that a band nearly 30 years into its career could still debut at number one worldwide. For the broader metal genre—in 2008 commercially overshadowed by hip-hop, pop, and alternative rock—*Death Magnetic*'s global chart performance demonstrated that thrash metal retained a substantial worldwide audience.

The Guitar Hero connection also placed Metallica's music before a new generation of younger listeners who encountered the band through interactive media, a cross-media exposure that contributed to metal's partial resurgence in the late 2000s and early 2010s — part of the broader [peak of rhythm gaming]({{ '/news/science-technology/rhythm-games-peak/' | relative_url }}) in this period.
