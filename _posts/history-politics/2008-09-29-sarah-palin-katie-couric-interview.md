**Key figures**: Sarah Palin (vice-presidential candidate), Katie Couric (CBS News anchor), John McCain, Barack Obama

## Summary

Between September 24 and October 1, 2008, CBS News anchor Katie Couric conducted a series of interviews with Alaska Governor Sarah Palin, Republican nominee John McCain's running mate. The first interview aired on September 24; subsequent segments followed through early October. In the interviews, Couric posed straightforward questions about foreign policy, economics, and Palin's qualifications for the vice presidency. Palin's responses—characterized by vague language, incomplete sentences, and apparent unfamiliarity with basic facts—were widely perceived as inadequate and damaging. The interviews became a cultural moment and a turning point in the 2008 campaign, with the exchanges replayed extensively on cable news and the internet, fundamentally altering the trajectory of Palin's candidacy.

The most memorable exchange involved Couric asking Palin what newspapers and magazines she read to stay informed about the world. Palin's response—"all of them"—proved evasive when pressed for specificity, and her follow-up explanations appeared to reveal gaps in her foreign-policy knowledge and media consumption. Other segments highlighted her difficulty articulating her position on the financial crisis, her understanding of the Federal Reserve's role, and her foreign-policy experience. Couric's interview style was straightforward and non-confrontational, which some media analysts noted made Palin's struggles appear more starkly evident by contrast. The interview series was broadcast during the acute phase of the 2008 financial crisis, a moment when voters were prioritizing economic competence and grasp of global affairs—areas where Palin appeared least prepared.

## The Interview Segments

The interview was conducted across three primary sessions, with CBS News airing different clips over multiple evenings of the *CBS Evening News with Katie Couric*:

- **September 24, 2008**: The first segment aired, featuring Palin's remarks on the financial crisis and her initial attempts to defend John McCain's decision to suspend his campaign. Palin expressed support for the federal bailout of financial institutions but struggled when pressed on its mechanisms.
- **September 25–26, 2008**: Subsequent clips aired featuring Palin's foreign-policy explanations, most notably her assertion that Alaska's proximity to Russia—"You can actually see Russia from land here in Alaska"—constituted foreign-policy experience. The clip became immediately viral and was widely ridiculed.
- **September 29, 2008 (broadcast)**: The most-discussed segment aired, in which Couric asked Palin about her media consumption. Palin's answer, that she read "all of them, any of them that have been in front of me over all these years," drew immediate mockery. The segment aired on the *CBS Evening News* and generated far greater reach online, with clips circulating widely through YouTube and early social media platforms.
- **October 1, 2008**: A final segment included Palin discussing abortion, contraception, and her views on Supreme Court decisions, where she struggled to name cases beyond *Roe v. Wade* when asked for examples.

An additional joint interview with John McCain on September 29 showed visible tension between the two candidates as Couric probed their differences on the financial crisis and Pakistan policy.

## Key Exchanges

**The Russia Exchange**: When asked how Alaska's proximity to Russia constituted foreign-policy experience, Palin responded: "They're our next-door neighbors and you can actually see Russia from land here in Alaska, from an island in Alaska." While geographically accurate in a narrow sense (the Diomede Islands are within sight of the Russian coast), the claim was widely interpreted as a non-answer on foreign-policy substance. Tina Fey's subsequent parody on *Saturday Night Live*, delivering the line as "I can see Russia from my house!"—words Palin never actually said—became more widely quoted than the original.

**The Newspaper Question**: Couric asked: "What newspapers and magazines did you regularly read before you were tapped for this—to stay informed and to understand the world?" Palin replied: "I've read most of them, again with a great appreciation for the press, for the media." When pressed for specific titles, she said: "All of them, any of them that have been in front of me over all these years." Couric's follow-up—"Can you name any of them?"—was answered with: "I have a vast variety of sources where we get our news." The exchange was universally interpreted as an inability to name a single news outlet.

**The Supreme Court Exchange**: When asked to name a Supreme Court decision she disagreed with besides *Roe v. Wade*, Palin struggled visibly and could not produce another example, saying, "Well, let's see. There's, of course, in the great history of America there have been rulings…"

## Cultural and Media Impact

The interviews triggered an immediate and outsized media reaction, with clips replaying across cable news and circulating widely online. *Saturday Night Live* seized on the material: on September 27, 2008, Tina Fey (as Palin) and Amy Poehler (as Couric) restaged the interview's most awkward exchanges almost verbatim in a sketch that became one of the season's most-watched segments and amplified the interview's cultural reach enormously. SNL's Palin sketches drove the show to its strongest ratings in years; the October 18 episode, on which Palin herself appeared alongside Fey, drew roughly 14 million viewers—the program's largest audience in some 14 years.

Katie Couric later won the Walter Cronkite Award for Excellence in Television Political Journalism for the interview series. The Couric–Palin exchanges were cited in multiple academic studies of political communication as a model of how brief, clip-able media moments could reshape national political discourse in the internet age.

## Significance

The Katie Couric interviews marked a watershed moment in the 2008 campaign. Before the interviews, Palin had enjoyed a significant "Palin Bounce" following her [August 29 selection as McCain's running mate](/news/history-politics/sarah-palin-vp-selection/)—McCain's Gallup polling had briefly pulled to parity with Obama following the Republican convention. The Couric interviews shifted media and public narrative toward questions about Palin's fitness for the vice presidency. Her perceived lack of preparedness became a constant line of attack for the Obama campaign and Democratic surrogates, and the interviews provided constant fodder for late-night comedy sketches, internet remixes, and news analysis.

An ABC News/Washington Post poll conducted in the days after the interviews showed Palin's favorable rating among independents dropping from 47% to 35%, one of the steepest post-interview swings for a vice-presidential candidate in modern polling history. By October, the financial crisis had intensified, McCain's campaign faced further headwinds, and Palin's gaffe-prone moment was cemented as a defining symbol of the ticket's final push toward defeat on November 4. The [2008 presidential debates](/news/history-politics/2008-presidential-debates/)—including Palin's October 2 debate with Joe Biden—were directly shaped by expectations set by the Couric interview: commentators noted that Palin had set such low expectations through the Couric sessions that even a modest debate performance was praised as a recovery.

The interviews also reflected a broader tension in 2008 between populist appeals and traditional qualifications. Palin's supporters framed the Couric interview as evidence of liberal media bias and an attempt to embarrass her, yet the unedited nature of the interviews made it difficult to sustain that narrative. The exchanges illustrated how a single, live, unscripted moment in the media age could rapidly reshape a national political trajectory. The Couric interviews became a reference point for discussions about vice-presidential readiness and media accountability in subsequent elections.
