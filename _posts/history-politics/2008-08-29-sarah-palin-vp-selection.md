## Summary

John McCain announced Alaska Governor Sarah Palin as his 2008 vice-presidential running mate on August 29, 2008, at a rally in Dayton, Ohio, making her the first female nominee on a Republican presidential ticket. The selection energized evangelical and social-conservative voters and generated immediate media attention, but the subsequent two months revealed significant gaps in Palin's preparation for high-level politics and foreign policy, ultimately undermining McCain's campaign message of readiness and experience. Despite the initial "Palin Bounce" in polling, gaffes, a Katie Couric interview that went viral for Palin's inability to name specific Supreme Court cases or publications she read, and internal campaign dysfunction eroded the ticket's momentum heading into the November 4 election.

## National Convention and Introduction

McCain introduced Palin to the nation the day after Barack Obama's climactic Denver convention speech, a deliberate counter-programming move designed to deflect post-convention media momentum. The announcement drew immediate international attention: Palin was barely known outside Alaska, and her selection was seen as audacious even by Republican Party insiders. At the Republican National Convention in Minneapolis–St. Paul (September 1–4, 2008), Palin's acceptance speech — written with the assistance of speechwriter Matthew Scully — was watched by an estimated 37.2 million viewers, matching Obama's convention speech audience and exceeding the viewership of Game 7 of the 2007 World Series. Her keynote address was widely described as a turning point in public perception: sharp, confident, and rhetorically effective, it temporarily silenced doubters and produced a significant spike in Republican enthusiasm.

## The "Palin Bounce" and Subsequent Erosion

In the week immediately following the Republican convention (September 5–12, 2008), multiple polls showed the McCain-Palin ticket pulling ahead of or tying the Obama-Biden ticket nationally. A USA Today/Gallup poll conducted September 5–7 gave McCain a 54–44% lead among likely voters — an outlier among post-convention surveys, but indicative of a shift of roughly 8–10 points from the pre-convention baseline. The bounce did not survive the onset of the September 2008 financial crisis. Lehman Brothers' collapse on September 15, 2008 (see [Lehman Brothers Collapse](/news/society-economics/lehman-brothers-collapse/)) shifted voter priorities decisively toward economic competence — an arena in which the McCain campaign struggled to articulate a consistent message. The subsequent [2008 financial crisis](/news/society-economics/2008-financial-crisis/) deepened the liability, and a CBS News/New York Times poll from October 2008 found 59% of likely voters describing Palin as "not ready to be president."

## Key Facts

- **Announcement:** August 29, 2008, Dayton, Ohio — the day after Obama's Democratic convention speech
- **Sarah Palin Background:** Governor of Alaska (December 2006–July 2009), Mayor of Wasilla, Alaska (1996–2002), Alaska Oil and Gas Conservation Commission member (2003–2004)
- **Age & Experience:** 44 years old at time of selection; approximately 20 months as Alaska Governor; zero federal government or foreign-policy experience
- **Nomination Reaction:** Republican convention speech watched by 37.2 million viewers; Palin Bounce peaked at ~8–10 points in September Gallup polls
- **Family Details:** Mother of five children — Track (deployed U.S. Army soldier), Bristol (18), Willow (14), Piper (7), and Trig (4 months; diagnosed with Down syndrome); husband Todd Palin, a commercial fisherman and former North Slope oil-field worker
- **Controversial Moments:**
  - **Katie Couric Interview (September 25–26, 2008):** Asked to name Supreme Court decisions she disagreed with beyond Roe v. Wade, Palin could not cite specific cases; asked to name publications she read regularly, she answered "all of them, any of them"; asked about Alaska's proximity to Russia as foreign-policy experience, she gave an answer that became a late-night comedy staple
  - **VP Debate (October 2, 2008 vs. Joe Biden):** Palin performed better than many anticipated after the Couric clips, drawing 69.9 million viewers (the largest VP debate audience in history), but Biden was widely judged the winner by post-debate polls
  - **Tina Fey's SNL parody:** Tina Fey's impression of Palin, premiering September 13, 2008, became a cultural phenomenon; multiple clips exceeded 10 million YouTube views within days, compounding the damage from the actual Couric interview
- **Electoral Impact:** McCain-Palin lost to Obama-Biden 365–173 in the Electoral College and 52.9%–45.7% in the popular vote; exit polls showed Obama winning 56% of female voters, suggesting Palin failed to convert Clinton primary voters as intended

## Campaign Context

McCain's VP selection came at a strategic inflection point. [Barack Obama](/news/history-politics/barack-obama-election/) had secured the Democratic nomination in June 2008 after a protracted primary against [Hillary Clinton](/news/history-politics/hillary-clinton-2008-campaign/), leaving roughly 18 million Clinton primary voters potentially in play. McCain's campaign, led by chief strategist Steve Schmidt, calculated that Palin could attract some share of disaffected Clinton supporters while simultaneously energizing religious conservatives who had shown tepid enthusiasm for McCain. The strategy addressed two real weaknesses in McCain's coalition: his historically cool relationship with the evangelical base and his difficulty projecting "change" against a historic Democratic nominee.

However, the gamble intensified scrutiny over McCain's judgment at a moment when his campaign's core message was national-security experience and readiness. The selection's risks materialized through three compounding factors:

1. **The September financial crisis:** The Lehman collapse on September 15 — just 17 days after Palin's introduction — shifted election dynamics toward economic competence; Palin had no federal economic policy experience
2. **Media vetting:** National political journalists were unfamiliar with Palin and aggressively investigated her record; the TrooperGate investigation (into whether Palin had pressured an official to fire her former brother-in-law) and questions about her per diem expense claims generated sustained negative coverage
3. **Debate prep vs. unscripted appearances:** Palin's debate performance (following intensive preparation at John McCain's Sedona, Arizona ranch) stood in contrast to her unscripted Couric interview, reinforcing impressions of selective coaching rather than organic mastery of national issues

## Broader Political Legacy

The Palin selection foreshadowed the Republican Party's post-2008 trajectory toward populism, anti-establishment identity politics, and skepticism of credentialed expertise. Her national debut marked the rise of a distinct style of conservative celebrity politics — later associated with the Tea Party movement (2009–2010), talk-radio and cable-news populism, and Donald Trump's 2016 campaign, which Palin endorsed in January 2016.

The [2008 presidential debates](/news/history-politics/2008-presidential-debates/) — including the VP debate between Palin and Biden — crystallized these dynamics. Biden's command of foreign policy (30+ years in the Senate, including chairman of the Foreign Relations Committee) highlighted the contrast McCain's campaign had hoped to obscure. Post-2008 analysis by campaign insiders, including Steve Schmidt in multiple interviews, described the selection as among the most consequential mistakes of the campaign.
