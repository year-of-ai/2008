## Summary

On October 19, 2008, Colin Powell, the retired four-star general and former Secretary of State under President George W. Bush (2001–2005), endorsed Democrat Barack Obama for president in a widely anticipated appearance on NBC's *Meet the Press*. Powell's endorsement was a watershed moment in the 2008 campaign, representing a significant crossing of Republican-Democratic lines at a pivotal moment less than three weeks before the November 4 general election. In the interview with moderator Tom Brokaw, Powell praised Obama as a "transformational figure" and expressed concern about the direction of the Republican Party under John McCain and Sarah Palin.

Powell's decision to publicly support Obama was framed around several core concerns. He criticized McCain's selection of Alaska Governor Sarah Palin as his running mate, questioning her readiness for the presidency; cited the economy and financial crisis as a moment requiring fresh thinking and unity; and expressed unease about what he characterized as the Republican Party's shift toward ideology and divisiveness. Powell had been a respected voice within the Republican establishment—his endorsement of the 2003 invasion of Iraq, though delivered at the United Nations, had given the administration diplomatic cover—and his 2008 break with party orthodoxy carried weight beyond typical political endorsements. Powell's stated reasoning focused on policy and character rather than party loyalty: he emphasized Obama's steadiness, intellectual depth, and ability to inspire, contrasting this with McCain's perceived erraticism, particularly regarding his September suspension of his campaign over the financial crisis.

## Background: Colin Powell's Republican History

Colin Powell served as National Security Advisor under President Reagan (1987–1989), Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff under Presidents George H.W. Bush and Bill Clinton (1989–1993), and Secretary of State under George W. Bush (2001–2005). His military career spanned four decades, culminating in the rank of four-star general. Despite persistent speculation about a possible presidential run in 1996—a Gallup poll that year showed him leading hypothetical race matchups by double digits—Powell had always remained publicly aligned with the Republican Party.

His most consequential act of Republican loyalty came on February 5, 2003, when he delivered a 76-minute address to the United Nations Security Council presenting intelligence evidence for Iraq's alleged weapons of mass destruction program—evidence that later proved inaccurate. Powell subsequently called the speech a "blot" on his record and it significantly colored the context of his 2008 endorsement: his willingness to break with party leadership carried implicit acknowledgment of his own past willingness to follow party leadership to his regret.

## The *Meet the Press* Appearance

The October 19 *Meet the Press* segment ran approximately 20 minutes. Powell delivered his endorsement in measured, deliberate language that stood in sharp contrast to typical partisan advocacy. Key elements of his statement:

- **On Obama's readiness**: Powell said Obama had shown "intellectual vigor" and "depth of knowledge" in their conversations, describing him as someone who had "met the standard of being a successful president, being an exceptional president."
- **On Sarah Palin**: Powell said directly that Palin was "not ready to be president of the United States, which is the job of the vice president." He cited her preparedness as a deciding factor in his choice.
- **On the Republican Party's direction**: Powell expressed concern about what he called the party's "narrowing" and its tendency to define itself around cultural wedge issues, its association of Islam with terrorism, and its shift away from fiscal discipline.
- **On the Muslim smear**: Powell explicitly addressed a persistent false rumor that Obama was Muslim, saying: "The correct answer is, he is not a Muslim, he's a Christian. He's always been a Christian. But the really right answer is, 'What if he is?'"—a reference to a 2008 photograph of a Muslim-American soldier buried at Arlington National Cemetery that Powell found emblematic of American pluralism.

The *Meet the Press* segment was broadcast on NBC. Within hours, video clips circulated across cable networks and online video platforms, making the endorsement one of the most widely viewed political moments of the closing weeks of the campaign.

## Polling Impact

The endorsement's effect on public opinion was measurable but bounded. Gallup tracking polls for the week of October 19–25 showed Obama maintaining approximately a 51%–44% lead over McCain, a margin that had opened in the weeks following the September financial crisis. Several independent analysts and news organizations noted the endorsement had the strongest effect on two voter groups:

- **Military veterans and national-security voters**: A subset that had trended Republican in previous cycles showed notable softening toward Obama in post-endorsement tracking, according to NBC/Wall Street Journal polling.
- **Moderate suburban independents**: The specific demographic Powell most credibly addressed—security-conscious, economically anxious suburban voters—showed a 4–6 point shift toward Obama in surveys conducted in the week after October 19, according to Pew Research Center tracking from late October 2008.

McCain campaign manager Rick Davis publicly downplayed the endorsement, saying it was "not surprising" given Powell and Obama's past relationship. McCain himself responded in interviews that he had the endorsements of "four former Secretaries of State"—a point that underscored the isolation of Powell's break with Republican foreign-policy orthodoxy.

## Significance

Powell's October 19 endorsement represented a rare moment of cross-party political realignment in a polarized era. As a senior Republican figure who had served in every post-Reagan conservative administration and whose credibility with military and national-security audiences was substantial, Powell's defection carried symbolic and practical weight. Beyond 2008, Powell's endorsement foreshadowed a broader Republican Party realignment: he endorsed Obama again in 2012, backed Hillary Clinton in 2016, and endorsed Joe Biden in 2020. Powell's trajectory embodied the fracturing of Cold War–era conservative consensus that accelerated over the following decade.

The endorsement also became a cultural touchstone for debates about party identity and the role of senior statesmen in political alignment. It demonstrated that even traditional Republican strongholds—military veterans, national-security professionals, foreign-policy elites—were fractured over the 2008 choice. The endorsement cemented Obama's late-campaign momentum, which had been building since the September financial crisis and the [Couric–Palin interviews](/news/history-politics/sarah-palin-katie-couric-interview/) that damaged public perception of Palin's qualifications.

The episode also resonated beyond electoral politics. Powell's direct address of the "What if he is a Muslim?" question—his most quoted remark from the endorsement—became a widely shared statement on religious pluralism in American public life, cited in editorial columns and academic work on the 2008 campaign for years afterward.
